# Worked example

Request: *"explain the building process using deep foundation timber-driven piles with
concrete pile caps and concrete grade beams."*

The explanation is below. Annotations in `[brackets]` name the section doing the work —
they are commentary for you, not part of the output.

---

`[1 — the problem. Opens on the failure, not on definitions. Note the specific consequence:
uneven settlement cracking things, not "serviceability concerns."]`

**The problem:** you want to build on ground that's basically pudding. Soft sand, muck,
peat, high water table — classic coastal Florida. If you set the building on that dirt it
sinks, and worse, it sinks *unevenly*, which cracks everything.

`[2 — the idea, in one repeatable sentence.]`

**The idea:** don't rest on the pudding. Reach through it, down to the firm stuff.

`[3 — steps. Each heading is plain language plus the body-part metaphor, and each step
answers "why does this exist."]`

### 1. Drive the piles — the legs

Long treated wood poles get hammered into the ground until they refuse to go deeper.

`[Vocabulary taught, not hidden — and the term is immediately cashed out as a mechanism the
reader can picture. "Refusal" is exactly the word they'd hear on a site.]`

The magic word is **refusal**. The driver counts hammer blows per inch. When the pile stops
moving meaningfully, it's either bearing on firm soil or held by friction along its whole
length, like a nail in wood. That blow count *is* the load test — you're not guessing
capacity, you're measuring it as you go.

### 2. Cut off the tops — make them even

Piles never stop at the same elevation, because the soil isn't uniform. So they're cut off
at one common elevation.

`[The non-obvious reason. This is the detail that makes the reader feel they learned
something real rather than something they could have guessed.]`

Where possible you cut *below* the permanent water level. Wood that stays fully submerged
doesn't rot — no oxygen, no fungus. Wood that cycles wet and dry is what rots.

### 3. Pour the pile caps — the feet

Now you have ragged wood tops sticking up, individually wobbly. A block of reinforced
concrete is formed over each cluster and poured, swallowing the pile tops a few inches.

That block is the **pile cap**. One job: take the concentrated load from the column above,
spread it evenly into the group of piles below, and lock those piles together so they act
as one unit instead of several independent sticks.

### 4. Pour the grade beams — the skeleton

`[5 — the misconception, placed inline where it does the most good rather than saved for
the end. This is the highest-value sentence in the whole explanation.]`

Here's the thing people get wrong: **a grade beam is a beam, not a footing.** It does not
rest on the dirt for support — the soil under it can settle away entirely and that's fine.
It spans between the caps. Its job is to carry the walls above, hand that load sideways to
the nearest pile caps, and tie the foundation into one rigid unit so the caps can't drift
or tilt independently.

`[Payoff — connects the mechanism to something the reader already cares about.]`

That last part is why the system is popular in Florida: the whole foundation behaves as one
rigid mat riding on legs, which is exactly what you want in a wind event.

### 5. Slab and up

The floor slab goes in — poured on fill between the grade beams, or, where soil is really
bad, as a **structural slab** that spans between the beams and doesn't touch the ground for
support at all.

`[4 — the mental model, set off so it survives when everything else fades.]`

---

### The mental model

> **Piles are the legs. Caps are the feet. Grade beams are the pelvis and spine tying the
> legs together. The soil at the surface holds up nothing.**

`[6 — trade-offs, because timber is one option among several. Honest about the limits
rather than selling it.]`

### Why timber specifically

Cheap, fast to drive, no rebar cage or cure time like concrete piles. Against it: lower
capacity per pile (so you use more), a practical length limit, marine borers in salt water,
and the water-table decay rule above. For low- to mid-rise on bad soil it's often the
economical answer.

---

## What makes this work

- Every step says **why**, so the reader can reconstruct the system instead of memorizing it.
- Real vocabulary — *refusal, pile cap, grade beam, structural slab* — is taught in plain
  words, so the reader can now talk to an engineer.
- The metaphor (legs / feet / spine) runs through every heading and lands in the mental
  model. It's used consistently, not sprinkled.
- The misconception gets its own beat, in bold, at the moment it's relevant.
- Nothing is asserted with more confidence than it deserves: "where possible", "often the
  economical answer".
